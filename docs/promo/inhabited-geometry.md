# Inhabited Geometry

*A visual philosophy.*

## The movement

Every dwelling is drawn before it is lived in. The plan comes first — orthogonal,
measured, indifferent — and only later does someone put a chair against that wall
and a birthday on that calendar. **Inhabited Geometry** works in the space between
those two states: it borrows the cold apparatus of the drafting table, the module
grid, the dimension line, the revision block, and uses it to describe things that
are warm and unmeasurable. The tension is the whole point. A plan sheet that quietly
concerns love, habit and belonging is stranger and more affecting than any
illustration of the same, and it must be executed with the patience of a draughtsman
who has spent a career at the board — every tick deliberate, every alignment earned.

## Space and form

The substrate is a module grid, always present, never announced: a faint lattice at
the smallest unit, firmer lines every eighth, so the eye senses order before it can
name it. Forms are **outlined, not filled**. A rectangle with corner ticks and an
open interior is more alive than a solid one — the viewer's eye closes the shape and
in doing so joins the work. Negative space holds the majority of the surface and is
never treated as leftover; it is the room the drawing describes. Nothing floats:
every element sits on the module, and where something must break the grid it does so
once, deliberately, and the break becomes the composition's pulse.

## Colour and material

A ground of near-black, the colour of a drafting table under a single lamp. On it,
one cool accent carries every trace of intent — the line that means *this is the
thing to notice* — and it is spent sparingly enough that its every appearance is an
event. Two further hues, an ochre and a muted green, exist only as punctuation: a
single dot, a two-millimetre rule, never a field. No gradients, no shadows, no
softness. Ink on ground. Material honesty means the marks look drawn rather than
rendered, and the restraint must read as the choice of someone who could have used
fifty colours and used three.

## Scale and rhythm

The sheet is governed by violent contrast of scale: one enormous form — a numeral, a
word — set against a field of whisper-small notation that rewards leaning in. The
large element carries the room; the small ones carry the hours. Repetition builds the
texture: ticks along a rule, dots in a column, hatch marks accumulating patiently
until they become tone rather than line. This density is the evidence of labour, and
it must be genuine — a hundred marks placed with care read differently from a hundred
marks generated, and the difference is visible at a glance to anyone who has made
things.

## Composition and hierarchy

A sheet reads in three moments: the **announcement** that stops the eye, the
**diagram** that holds it, and the **record** that closes it — a title block, set in
the smallest type on the page, carrying the dry facts. Leader lines and dimension
arrows do the work that explanation would otherwise do; they point, and the pointing
is the sentence. Text appears only as label, measure and mark: a handful of words,
each earning its place, none of them explaining what the geometry already says. If a
paragraph seems necessary, the composition has failed and must be redrawn.

## The standard

This is not decoration applied to information. It is a made object, and it must
survive the scrutiny of someone who draws for a living: no element clipped, no label
colliding, no arrow ending short of its mark, margins consistent to the pixel. The
finished piece should look like the product of many quiet hours by a practitioner at
the top of their craft — meticulous, unhurried, unmistakably human in its judgement
and inhuman in its precision. Anything less is a draft, and drafts are not shown.
